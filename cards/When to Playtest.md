# Why playtest...

During college, I learned a lot about playtesting, which was heavily emphasized within the game design and development culture at RIT. In principle, I understand its value. It's an opportunity to gather feedback, observe how people interact with your work, get an early impression of potential collaborators, and even explore whether there's an audience for what you're building. With how picky people can be today, product-market fit can clearly signal whether something stays a portfolio piece or warrants the budget to take risks.

In practice, however, I've become increasingly skeptical of how playtests are conducted and how their results are interpreted.

I think of part of this as the _Yelp problem_. When people are asked to critique something, they sometimes become more concerned with playing the role of a critic than describing their actual experience. That doesn't necessarily make their feedback dishonest or malicious, but it introduces a lot of noise. A playtest can quickly become a collection of personal preferences, hypothetical improvements, and suggestions that have little to do with what you're trying to evaluate.

This becomes especially frustrating when a playtest has a specific objective. You might be trying to evaluate one mechanic or interaction, only to spend most of the session discussing features unrelated to the question you originally wanted answered. Those observations can still be useful, but it's frustrating when they take over the session and leave the original question unanswered.

The other challenge is indifference. Responses like "it's fine" or "I don't know" tell you very little on their own, particularly when you're trying to understand why something did or didn't work. It's tempting to keep asking questions until you get a more definitive reaction, but that risks leading the player toward an answer they might not have given otherwise. At the same time, treating indifference as approval isn't particularly useful either.

Arguing over taste is another difficult part of the process. There's almost always someone outside your serviceable addressable market who's convinced they know how to expand your player base. Their suggestions aren't necessarily bad, but there's something frustrating about someone with little interest in the experience you're building insisting they know how to make it appeal to a wider audience. These conversations can quickly turn into debates over what the game should become rather than what you're actually trying to test.

## Does money solve all problems?

Yes and yes... well, to an extent.

Working in QA has made me realize just how effective money can be as a motivator. Paying someone doesn't necessarily make their feedback more honest or insightful, but it can make the process more dependable. There's a clearer agreement about the tasks, time, and attention the session requires.

The other half of the problem is managing the playtest itself. For a smaller team, framing a session and sorting through feedback can become a project of its own. I think the tools available today make that process more manageable and less of a risk. They don't replace the judgment needed to interpret results, but they can reduce the time spent organizing feedback and make regular testing less overwhelming.

I've also started thinking more about what I can learn before bringing people in. Practices from UX testing, such as using feature flags, session recordings, and analytics, can help answer specific questions as a game develops. If I want to compare how often a mechanic is used or how two implementations behave, I can gather some of that information internally. That gives me a better starting point when I do ask people to play.

I still think qualitative playtests, where I can watch people play and hear about their experience, are valuable at milestones. They help me step back and see how the game feels to someone who hasn't been working on it every day. There’s also nothing wrong with getting people together to play because if you enjoy the social side of making games.
